01 / About me

Building technology means understanding the human and field problem first.

I am , an AI Engineer, Software Developer and Africa Agricultural Researcher with a strong interest in the practical relationship between technology, evidence and development. I work across software engineering, artificial intelligence, agricultural research, drone-enabled data collection, geospatial information and digital systems because many of Africa’s most important problems do not fit neatly inside one technical discipline.

My philosophy is rooted in problem definition before implementation. A promising idea becomes valuable only when it addresses a clearly understood need, when the information required to operate it can be collected reliably, and when the resulting output can be understood and acted upon by the people who need it. I therefore think about technology as a complete chain: problem → data → workflow → intelligence → decision → measurable outcome.

A central area of my work is agricultural intelligence. Farming is inherently spatial, seasonal and information-intensive. A field can contain variation in crop condition, soil context, moisture, plant density, pest or disease pressure and management history. I am interested in how imagery, computer vision, remote sensing, mapping, structured field records and analytics can transform scattered observations into organized information that supports monitoring, research and operational decisions.

Through Agri-Technology Africa, I am developing a technology direction that combines agricultural drone services, AI-assisted analysis and a digital agriculture platform. The long-term concept is to build an information layer around farm operations: capture observations, preserve useful records, analyze imagery and field data, generate structured reports, and make information easier to revisit over time. This creates a foundation for more consistent agricultural services rather than isolated one-time observations.

I also place strong emphasis on responsible AI. Agricultural AI should not be presented as an unquestionable authority. Image-based systems can encounter changing lighting, crop varieties, field conditions, incomplete datasets and situations outside their training or validation environment. For that reason, AI-assisted findings should be treated as decision-support indicators until they have been appropriately tested, compared with field observations and reviewed by relevant experts. My preferred development cycle is evidence-driven: build, test, measure, document limitations, learn from failure and improve.

My broader ambition is to contribute to an African technology ecosystem in which locally relevant data is treated as strategic infrastructure. Better structured agricultural information can strengthen farm management, research, service delivery, planning and future innovation. I want to build systems that are technically credible while remaining understandable, practical and adaptable to the realities of the people and organizations expected to use them.

Over time, I hope to deepen this work through stronger field validation, partnerships, research collaboration and scalable digital infrastructure across Ghana and wider Africa. The objective is not simply to demonstrate that technology can be built; it is to demonstrate that carefully designed technology can become useful, trusted and measurable in the environments where it matters most.

04 / Research direction

African agriculture deserves technology designed around its realities, constraints and opportunities.

My research direction is concerned with the gap between what agricultural technology can theoretically do and what can be responsibly delivered in the field. African farming environments are diverse; data availability, connectivity, farm size, crop systems, operating costs and local expertise can vary substantially. Technology therefore has to be adaptable, evidence-led and designed with the operational environment in mind.

Rather than starting with a model and searching for a problem, I prefer to start with an observable agricultural need and work backward toward the minimum information required to address it. This makes validation central to the process and helps distinguish a useful indicator from an unsupported claim.

01ObserveDefine the field problem and document what can actually be seen or measured.
02MeasureCollect consistent, structured information with context, location and time.
03ModelUse AI and analytics where they add evidence, efficiency or useful pattern recognition.
04ValidateCompare outputs with controlled observations and appropriate expert review.
05CommunicateExplain confidence, limitations and practical meaning rather than hiding uncertainty.
06ImproveUse field feedback and new evidence to refine the system before broader deployment.

06 / Working philosophy

01

Build with purpose.

A sophisticated interface is not the same thing as a useful system. I begin with the underlying problem, the people affected by it, the information they need and the decision the technology is expected to improve. Every technical choice should serve that purpose.

02

Evidence before confidence.

Especially in AI and agriculture, impressive outputs can be misleading when they are not properly validated. I believe in distinguishing observations from predictions, predictions from interpretations, and prototypes from proven field performance. Measurement and expert review should shape trust.

03

Think Africa-first, build for scale.

Technology should respect local operating realities while remaining ambitious. Solutions should consider connectivity, affordability, skills, infrastructure, data quality and field workflows without assuming that African users must accept lower standards of quality or innovation.
